Dispatch 4.2 Review

On 12 August we gave responders 60 seconds instead of 90 to answer a callout. Missed pings jumped from 2% to 18% that day. Because a missed ping is scored as a "no", four responders slid to the bottom of the line and almost stopped getting work. Neighbours took their areas, and twice as many callouts now go unfilled.

What happened

Missed pings jumped on release day. The average then hid the damage.

Acceptance looks like it is recovering, from 54% in release week to 73% by the week of 31 August. That recovery is not real. Four responders are now barely pinged, so their misses drop out of the average. Their acceptance since 24 August is zero.

Who it happened to

Four responders starved. Seven took on their work.

Also opened with two misses, then took three in a row and recovered. Went from 13 to 21 pings a week and took over Eastgate from Meteor Mite, who shares the same area and handler.

Why it happened

How a shorter wait became a trap

Before 4.2 the quiet four and the busiest five were indistinguishable: about 12 pings a week, 75–77% accepted, 3% missed, about 23 seconds to turn a ping down. Then:

  1. The window shrank to 60 seconds.Missed offers now end at 61–63 s, against 91–93 s before. Turning down still takes about 22 s, so deciding is not the problem; reaching the phone is.
  2. The quiet four missed half their pings in the first week.18 of 36 (50%) against 11 of 74 (15%) for the busiest five. Even on jobs in their own area they missed 38%, against 5%. Likely cause: they usually needed 60–90 s to reach the phone (inferred; accept times are not recorded).
  3. Each miss counted as a "no".−0.12 per miss, +0.08 per yes. Taking under 60% of pings drives a score down. The four took 18–29%.
  4. A lower score handed their area to a neighbour.Under 0.3, responders got 1.4 pings a week, against 13 at 0.9+. Everyone who stayed at 0.44 or above recovered; nobody at 0.28 or below with a stronger neighbour did.
  5. Silence made the next ping easier to miss.After 3+ days without a ping, 73% of pings were missed, against 0% before 4.2. The score hit zero and cannot recover on its own.

What it is not: time of day, weekday, position in the queue, part of the city, or responder quality. Missed pings rose in every split, and before 4.2 the four took 69–81% of their pings (Vesper was third best of 16). The change to rank nearby responders higher had a small, short-lived effect and actually softened the trap.

How we found it

Process

STEP 1

Interviews

Read all four handler interviews. Grouped complaints, counted, noted which were volunteered.

STEP 2

Tickets

Read all 147 ticket bodies. Themes by week, responder, open or closed. Found who never files.

STEP 3

Pings

Outcomes before and after, per responder, gaps, unfilled callouts. Ruled out time, place, queue position.

STEP 4

Code

Traced the routing rules. Replayed every ping through the scoring to see who fell, and why some recovered.

STEP 5

Cross-check

Checked ticket claims against pings, compared quiet against busy responders, and tested alert rules.

Recommendations

Fix the timing and the scoring, not the ranking

Keep the change to rank nearby responders higher. It was a long-standing responder request, and the data shows it is not the cause.

Why fixing it matters

Every week this waits, the product fails at its one job more often

  • More incidents with no responder. Callouts nobody took doubled from 5% to 11%. Dispatch exists to get someone there.
  • We are losing good responders. The four took 69–81% of their pings before 4.2. Two are now asking if they are "still even in the system". Dispatch is the reason responders stay with Rook.
  • The busy ones are being worn down. Seven responders took on 2–6 more pings a week, with acceptance falling to about 70%. Their own areas suffer: Southport went from 4% to 22% unfilled.
  • Handlers have stopped hearing back. 45 callout tickets are open, some for four weeks. The two most affected handlers filed every two or three days.
  • It will not fix itself. Scores do not recover without pings, and the starved responders barely get any. Waiting for September to "come back" leaves them at zero.
  • The headline number hides it. Average acceptance is climbing back while the four sit at zero. Without per-responder reporting, the next release could do the same thing unseen.
